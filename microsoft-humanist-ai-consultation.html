Humanist AI Needs a Theory of What Alignment Is For

Response to Microsoft AI's Humanist AI Code of Conduct consultation

Nicholas Clarke, PhD
Independent Researcher in Philosophy and AI Alignment
September 2026

This response was prepared as part of Microsoft AI's public consultation on its Humanist AI Code of Conduct . It develops a proposal for strengthening the Code's treatment of human flourishing, value pluralism, and alignment-target specification.

Microsoft AI's Humanist AI Code of Conduct is a welcome attempt to state explicitly what increasingly capable AI systems should be for, how they should behave, and where their limits should lie. I am particularly sympathetic to its insistence that advanced AI should remain subordinate to human beings: corrigible, interruptible, bounded in scope, and supportive of human agency rather than a substitute for it.

The Code also acknowledges something equally important: concepts such as "human flourishing" remain difficult to make sufficiently concrete and evaluable. Microsoft explicitly asks how to better cement the right values in its models, how to make human flourishing more concrete, and what metrics should be used to evaluate it.

These questions point towards a distinction that I think should become more explicit in the Humanist AI framework: the distinction between the control problem and the alignment-target problem.

The control problem concerns how we ensure that increasingly capable systems remain under meaningful human authority. It encompasses corrigibility, shutdownability, transparency, containment, scope limitation, oversight, and restrictions on dangerous actions. The Code has a comparatively developed answer to this problem.

The alignment-target problem is different. It asks: what should a successfully controlled AI ultimately be helping humans to achieve?

"Human flourishing" is an attractive answer, but it is not yet a sufficiently determinate one. People disagree about what flourishing involves, how its components should be weighted, what should happen when autonomy conflicts with welfare or safety, and which human preferences should be treated as authoritative. These difficulties become increasingly consequential as AI systems become better able to advise, persuade, plan, and act.

The two problems cannot simply be collapsed into one another. Even a perfectly controllable AI could faithfully pursue an inadequately specified objective. Conversely, identifying a well-grounded alignment target would not eliminate the need for technical control, interpretability, corrigibility, and governance.

Humanist AI therefore needs both.

From current preference to reflectively informed preference

One way to make the target side of alignment more tractable is to distinguish what people currently want from what they would continue to want under improved conditions of information and reflection.

Actual human preferences are not automatically good guides to human flourishing. They can depend upon misinformation, ignorance of consequences, framing effects, cognitive biases, inconsistency, social pressure, or temporary emotional states. People routinely revise important preferences when they acquire relevant information or have an opportunity to reflect.

An AI that becomes increasingly effective at satisfying preferences as currently expressed could therefore become increasingly effective at helping people obtain outcomes they would subsequently reject.

This suggests a different candidate alignment target: idealised human desire—roughly, what people would most strongly want under conditions of improved information, rational deliberation, and greater coherence across time and contexts.

This idea should not be interpreted as constructing an abstract "ideal self" whose judgement overrides that of an actual person. Nor should an AI system decide independently what humans ought to value.

Instead, idealisation can be incremental, transparent, and contestable. AI might help a person understand relevant facts, explore likely consequences, identify inconsistencies, encounter serious counterarguments, consider neglected perspectives, or test whether a judgement changes under alternative framings. Human beings would remain responsible for assessing and endorsing the results.

That conception fits naturally with one of the strongest themes in the Humanist AI Code: AI should improve human reasoning and informed decision-making while preserving human ownership of consequential choices.

The model's function is not to replace evaluation but to improve the epistemic conditions under which evaluation occurs.

Moral discovery rather than value imposition

This way of framing the problem suggests that alignment should partly be understood as a process of moral or evaluative discovery.

That phrase need not presuppose that there is a single objectively true moral theory waiting to be uncovered. The more modest claim is that there are discoverable facts about how human values change—or remain stable—when identifiable epistemic deficiencies are reduced.

For example, some preferences disappear when people discover that they rested on false factual beliefs. Some become more stable after consideration of long-term consequences. Others survive extensive reflection and exposure to competing arguments.

Those transformations contain information relevant to alignment.

Rather than deciding in advance that utilitarianism, deontology, virtue ethics, or some particular account of human flourishing is correct, an empirical process could investigate which human evaluative commitments withstand improved information and deliberation.

Such a process would remain fallible. Better information tomorrow might alter conclusions reached today. Different methods of reflection might produce different results. The appropriate response is therefore not to freeze the output into an immutable value function, but to make alignment targets revisable.

The resulting picture is less like discovering a final moral constitution and more like maintaining an improving model of what human beings have reason, by their own considered lights, to want.

Pluralism should be an empirical result

This approach also offers a way of sharpening the Code's commitment to plural values.

Pluralism should neither be assumed away nor treated as meaning that every existing preference must simply be accepted.

Some apparent disagreement is driven by factual misunderstanding, misinformation, framing, or confusion. Better epistemic conditions may reduce it.

Other disagreement may survive extensive information and reflection. That is importantly different.

We could therefore distinguish distorted disagreement from reflectively stable pluralism.

If disagreement persists when participants understand the relevant evidence, appreciate the consequences of different choices, confront serious alternatives, and have opportunities for sustained reflection, that persistence is itself evidence about the structure of human values.

An aligned system should not manufacture convergence where none exists. It should represent residual disagreement, communicate uncertainty, allow different legitimate priorities to remain visible, and preserve space for individual choice and appropriate governance mechanisms.

Idealisation is therefore compatible with pluralism rather than a mechanism for eliminating it.

This can be empirically investigated

The most important advantage of this approach is that it need not remain purely philosophical.

Microsoft explicitly recognises that evaluation of human flourishing is still a nascent field. An idealisation framework suggests a complementary class of empirical measures.

Researchers could investigate, for example, whether preferences remain stable after participants receive relevant information; whether they survive changes in framing; whether structured reflection reduces subsequent regret; whether judgements remain stable following exposure to strong counterarguments; whether people continue to endorse decisions after consequences become clearer; and which areas display convergence or persistent disagreement across repeated deliberation.

These are not infallible measures of value. Nor should any single stability metric become another optimisation proxy. They are pieces of defeasible evidence.

But collectively they could allow alignment research to move beyond two unsatisfactory extremes: simply reproducing preferences as currently expressed, or imposing a fully specified conception of the good in advance.

The objective would instead be to approximate progressively what humans would endorse under epistemically improved conditions.

That could also enrich Microsoft's emerging Humanist AI evaluation programme. Behavioural evaluations ask whether a system exhibits desired characteristics such as transparency or respect for autonomy. A complementary upstream evaluation programme could ask whether the values and trade-offs encoded within those behavioural expectations themselves remain defensible under informed and reflective scrutiny.

Epistemic integrity becomes a safety property

There is a further implication.

If alignment depends partly on discovering what humans would endorse under improved epistemic conditions, then the integrity of those conditions becomes central to alignment itself.

An AI system can manipulate users without issuing explicit commands. It can selectively present evidence, frame alternatives asymmetrically, omit inconvenient considerations, conceal uncertainty, repeatedly reinforce particular preferences, or narrow the apparent space of available options.

As systems become more capable and persuasive, these forms of epistemic influence could become increasingly important.

A Humanist AI safety framework should therefore protect not only the user's final ability to choose, but the quality of the process through which that choice was formed.

This gives honesty, transparency, and corrigibility an additional role.

If a system distorts the evidence through which human values are being clarified, it undermines its own ability to identify a good alignment target. If it resists correction when new information changes our understanding of that target, it frustrates the process it is meant to support.

This does not mean that idealised-desire alignment automatically solves deception or guarantees corrigibility. External safeguards, interpretability, monitoring, containment, and governance remain essential.

The more limited point is that a discovery-oriented alignment target makes epistemic integrity constitutive of successful alignment rather than merely an additional behavioural rule.

A corrigibility-first architecture

It is especially important that none of this be interpreted as recommending an autonomous AI system charged with maximising its estimate of idealised human desire.

That would introduce precisely the risks of goal entrenchment and loss of human control that Humanist AI seeks to avoid.

Value discovery should instead operate inside a corrigibility-first architecture.

AI systems can act as epistemic and deliberative assistants: modelling consequences, identifying inconsistencies, exploring counterfactuals, representing uncertainty, and helping humans understand how their priorities might change under better conditions.

They should not acquire independent authority to implement some inferred conception of human flourishing against human judgement.

Discovery and deployment should remain separate. Human beings and legitimate governance processes retain authority over how evidence about idealised preferences is interpreted and used.

This division of labour combines two important ideas: AI can help us reason about values at scales and levels of complexity that would otherwise be difficult, while humans retain normative and political authority over what is done with the results.

Extending Humanist AI

I am developing this general approach in a research programme on AI-assisted moral discovery, which investigates whether idealised human desires can be progressively approximated through measurable improvements in information, reasoning, and coherence; how evaluative judgements change under those conditions; whether convergence occurs in some domains while stable pluralism persists in others; and whether these processes can provide more robust alignment targets than uncorrected behavioural preferences.

The proposal is not that this supplies a complete theory of human flourishing. Rather, it supplies a methodology for making the concept progressively more informative without prematurely resolving fundamental moral disagreements by stipulation.

Humanist AI already contains many of the ingredients required for such an approach: human primacy, corrigibility, agency, informed decision-making, pluralism, transparency, empirical evaluation, and an acknowledgement that flourishing remains an open research problem.

The next step could be to connect them through an explicit theory of alignment-target discovery.

The challenge of alignment is not only to teach powerful systems to follow the right rules. It is also to develop better ways of discovering, testing, and revising what those rules are ultimately meant to protect and promote.

If Humanist AI develops that second dimension alongside its strong commitments on control, it could move the alignment debate beyond the choice between reproducing existing preferences and imposing predetermined values.

It could instead help establish a genuinely human-centred process in which AI improves our capacity to understand what we value, humans retain authority over the conclusions, disagreement remains visible rather than artificially eliminated, and the target of alignment itself becomes progressively more informed, testable, and revisable.

Nicholas Clarke, PhD is an independent researcher working on AI alignment, moral philosophy, and the empirical study of idealised human preferences.
