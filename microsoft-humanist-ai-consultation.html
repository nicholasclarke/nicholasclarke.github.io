Humanist AI Needs a Theory of What Alignment Is For

Response to Microsoft AI's Humanist AI Code of Conduct consultation

Nicholas Clarke, PhD
Independent Researcher in Philosophy and AI Alignment
September 2026

This response was prepared as part of Microsoft AI's public consultation on its Humanist AI Code of Conduct . It develops a proposal for strengthening the Code's treatment of human flourishing, value pluralism, and alignment-target specification.

Microsoft AI's Humanist AI Code of Conduct is a welcome attempt to state explicitly what increasingly capable AI systems should be for, how they should behave, and where their limits should lie. I am particularly sympathetic to its insistence that advanced AI should remain subordinate to human beings: corrigible, interruptible, bounded in scope, and supportive of human agency rather than a substitute for it.

The Code also recognises an equally important difficulty. Concepts such as "human flourishing" are hard to make sufficiently concrete and evaluable. Microsoft explicitly asks how to better cement the right values in its models, how to make human flourishing more concrete, and what metrics should be used to evaluate it.

I think these questions point to a distinction that should be made more explicit within the Humanist AI framework: the distinction between the control problem and the alignment-target problem.

The control problem concerns how we ensure that increasingly capable systems remain under meaningful human authority. It encompasses corrigibility, shutdownability, transparency, containment, limits on scope, oversight, and restrictions on dangerous actions. The Code already has a comparatively developed answer to this problem.

The alignment-target problem is different. It asks what a successfully controlled AI should ultimately be helping humans to achieve.

"Human flourishing" is an attractive answer, but it is not yet a sufficiently determinate one. People disagree about what flourishing involves, how its components should be weighted, what should happen when autonomy conflicts with welfare or safety, and which human preferences should be treated as authoritative. These questions become more important as AI systems become increasingly capable of advising, persuading, planning, and acting.

The two problems should not be collapsed into one another. Even a perfectly controllable AI could faithfully pursue an inadequately specified objective. At the same time, identifying a well-grounded alignment target would not remove the need for technical control, interpretability, corrigibility, and governance.

Humanist AI therefore needs an account of both.

From current preference to reflectively informed preference

One way to make the target side of alignment more tractable is to distinguish between what people currently want and what they would continue to want under better conditions of information and reflection.

Actual human preferences are not automatically good guides to human flourishing. They can depend on misinformation, ignorance of consequences, framing effects, cognitive biases, inconsistency, social pressure, or temporary emotional states. People routinely revise important preferences when they learn more about a situation or have time to reflect on it.

An AI that becomes increasingly effective at satisfying preferences as currently expressed could therefore become increasingly effective at helping people obtain outcomes they would later reject.

A different candidate alignment target is idealised human desire: roughly, what people would most strongly want under conditions of improved information, rational deliberation, and greater coherence across time and contexts.

This should not be understood as constructing an abstract "ideal self" whose judgement overrides that of an actual person. Nor should an AI system decide independently what humans ought to value.

Idealisation can instead be incremental, transparent, and open to challenge. AI might help a person understand relevant facts, explore likely consequences, identify inconsistencies, encounter serious counterarguments, consider neglected perspectives, or test whether a judgement changes when a question is framed differently. Human beings would remain responsible for assessing and endorsing the results.

This fits naturally with one of the strongest themes in the Humanist AI Code: AI should improve human reasoning and support informed decision-making while preserving human ownership of consequential choices.

The model's role is not to replace evaluation, but to improve the conditions under which evaluation takes place.

Moral discovery rather than value imposition

Seen in this way, alignment becomes partly a process of moral or evaluative discovery.

That phrase need not assume that there is a single objectively true moral theory waiting to be uncovered. The more modest claim is that there are facts we can investigate about how human values change, or remain stable, when identifiable epistemic deficiencies are reduced.

Some preferences disappear when people discover that they rested on false factual beliefs. Some become more stable after people consider their long-term consequences. Others survive extensive reflection and exposure to competing arguments.

Those patterns of change and stability are relevant to alignment.

Instead of deciding in advance that utilitarianism, deontology, virtue ethics, or some particular account of human flourishing is correct, an empirical process could investigate which human evaluative commitments survive improved information and deliberation.

Any such process would remain fallible. Better information tomorrow might alter conclusions reached today, and different methods of reflection might sometimes produce different results. For that reason, the aim should not be to freeze the output into an immutable value function. Alignment targets should remain open to revision.

The picture is therefore less one of discovering a final moral constitution and more one of developing an increasingly good model of what human beings have reason, by their own considered lights, to want.

Pluralism should be an empirical result

This approach can also sharpen the Code's commitment to plural values.

Pluralism should not simply be assumed away, but neither should it be taken to mean that every existing preference must be accepted as it stands.

Some apparent disagreement is driven by factual misunderstanding, misinformation, framing, or confusion. Better epistemic conditions may reduce it.

Other disagreements may survive extensive information and reflection. That is an important difference.

We can therefore distinguish distorted disagreement from reflectively stable pluralism.

If disagreement persists when participants understand the relevant evidence, appreciate the consequences of different choices, confront serious alternatives, and have opportunities for sustained reflection, that persistence is itself evidence about the structure of human values.

An aligned system should not manufacture convergence where none exists. It should represent residual disagreement, communicate uncertainty, keep different legitimate priorities visible, and preserve room for individual choice and appropriate governance.

Idealisation, on this account, is compatible with pluralism. It is not a mechanism for eliminating it.

This can be investigated empirically

A major advantage of this approach is that it need not remain purely philosophical.

Microsoft explicitly recognises that the evaluation of human flourishing is still a nascent field. An idealisation framework suggests a complementary class of empirical measures.

Researchers could investigate whether preferences remain stable after participants receive relevant information; whether they survive changes in framing; whether structured reflection reduces subsequent regret; whether judgements remain stable after exposure to strong counterarguments; whether people continue to endorse decisions after the consequences become clearer; and which areas show convergence or persistent disagreement across repeated deliberation.

None of these would be an infallible measure of value, and no single stability metric should be allowed to become just another optimisation proxy. They would instead provide pieces of defeasible evidence.

Taken together, however, they could help alignment research move beyond two unsatisfactory alternatives: simply reproducing preferences as currently expressed, or imposing a fully specified conception of the good in advance.

The aim would be to approximate, progressively and cautiously, what humans would endorse under better epistemic conditions.

This could also enrich Microsoft's emerging Humanist AI evaluation programme. Behavioural evaluations ask whether a system exhibits desired characteristics such as transparency or respect for autonomy. A complementary upstream programme could ask whether the values and trade-offs built into those behavioural expectations themselves remain defensible under informed and reflective scrutiny.

Epistemic integrity as a safety property

This has an important consequence for safety.

If alignment depends partly on discovering what humans would endorse under improved epistemic conditions, then protecting the integrity of those conditions becomes part of alignment itself.

An AI system can manipulate users without issuing explicit commands. It can selectively present evidence, frame alternatives asymmetrically, omit inconvenient considerations, conceal uncertainty, repeatedly reinforce particular preferences, or narrow the range of options that appear worth considering.

As systems become more capable and persuasive, these forms of epistemic influence could become increasingly significant.

A Humanist AI safety framework should therefore protect more than the user's final ability to choose. It should also protect the quality of the process through which that choice was formed.

This gives honesty, transparency, and corrigibility an additional importance.

If a system distorts the evidence through which human values are being clarified, it makes itself worse at identifying a good alignment target. If it resists correction when new information changes our understanding of that target, it frustrates the process it is meant to support.

This does not mean that idealised-desire alignment automatically solves deception or guarantees corrigibility. External safeguards, interpretability, monitoring, containment, and governance remain essential.

The narrower claim is that a discovery-oriented alignment target makes epistemic integrity part of successful alignment itself, rather than treating it merely as an additional behavioural rule.

A corrigibility-first architecture

None of this should be interpreted as a proposal for an autonomous AI system charged with maximising its own estimate of idealised human desire.

That would introduce precisely the risks of goal entrenchment and loss of human control that Humanist AI is intended to avoid.

Value discovery should instead operate inside a corrigibility-first architecture.

AI systems can act as epistemic and deliberative assistants. They can model consequences, identify inconsistencies, explore counterfactuals, represent uncertainty, and help people understand how their priorities might change under better conditions.

They should not acquire independent authority to implement some inferred conception of human flourishing against human judgement.

Discovery and deployment should remain separate. Human beings and legitimate governance processes should retain authority over how evidence about idealised preferences is interpreted and used.

There is a natural division of labour here. AI can help us reason about values at scales and levels of complexity that would otherwise be difficult. Humans should retain normative and political authority over what is done with the results.

Extending Humanist AI

I am developing this general approach in a research programme on AI-assisted moral discovery. The programme investigates whether idealised human desires can be progressively approximated through measurable improvements in information, reasoning, and coherence; how evaluative judgements change under those conditions; whether convergence occurs in some domains while stable pluralism persists in others; and whether these processes can provide more robust alignment targets than uncorrected behavioural preferences.

The proposal is not that this gives us a complete theory of human flourishing. Rather, it offers a methodology for making the concept progressively more informative without trying to settle fundamental moral disagreements by stipulation.

Humanist AI already contains many of the ingredients needed for this approach: human primacy, corrigibility, agency, informed decision-making, pluralism, transparency, empirical evaluation, and an acknowledgement that flourishing remains an open research problem.

The next step could be to connect these elements through an explicit theory of alignment-target discovery.

The challenge of alignment is not just to teach powerful systems to follow the right rules. We also need better ways of discovering, testing, and revising what those rules are ultimately meant to protect and promote.

If Humanist AI develops that second dimension alongside its strong commitments on control, it could move the alignment debate beyond the choice between reproducing existing preferences and imposing predetermined values.

A more promising alternative is a genuinely human-centred process in which AI helps us understand what we value, humans retain authority over the conclusions, disagreement remains visible rather than being artificially eliminated, and the target of alignment itself becomes progressively better informed, more testable, and more open to revision.

Nicholas Clarke, PhD is an independent researcher working on AI alignment, moral philosophy, and the empirical study of idealised human preferences.
